\begin{abstract}
Agents act on facts about their user they were never told, such as whether a
scan is safe today, and nothing external can check them. We show that
sampling-consistency detectors cannot tell users apart within a
conversation, and bound how far they rise above chance by how often
resamples disagree with wrong and with right answers. We propose USHER
(User-Specific Hallucination Estimation from Reactions): the agent states
each assumption as a commitment with a confidence, the user's implicit
reactions are recorded, and a posterior turns both into per-commitment and
per-session risk. In a pre-registered evaluation with five LLM agents,
SelfCheckGPT and semantic entropy score
\pt{C/L_U/pooled/H1: AUROC(SelfCheckGPT)} and
\pt{C/L_U/pooled/H1: AUROC(semantic_entropy)} AUROC. An intake-aware
posterior, registered after the first results and run once, reaches
\pt{V/hc/L_U/pooled/--: AUROC(V7)}, beats a supervised detector on the same
inputs by \val{pa:V/hc/L_U/pooled/H8: V7 - intake_lr+fb} (one given its
structure matches it), and identifies which user-specific commitment is
wrong at \pt{PC/user_dependent/pooled/auroc_V7} AUROC, where resample
disagreement reaches \pt{PC/user_dependent/pooled/auroc_disagreement}.
\chk{In a closed loop amended after a first picker failed, asking about its
riskiest commitment cuts user-specific errors by
\val{CL7/reduction/V7 posterior@1}\%, beating a random question.} Exact
Bayes predictors show the reactions carry information beyond population
risk that resampling ignores. We report every registered outcome, including
failures.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} LLM agents; hallucination detection; uncertainty; clarification; implicit feedback.

\section{Introduction}

An agent that schedules imaging appointments must decide whether a patient
may have a contrast scan today. The intake form says the patient had a
recent treatment, but not when. A careful agent would say it does not
know; many instead answer ``safe, $p=0.85$'' and proceed. We call this a
\emph{user-specific hallucination}: a confident assertion about the current
user whose truth depends on something the user never disclosed. It differs
from the factual hallucinations that dominate the literature
\citep{ji2023survey}: there is no document or search engine to check it
against.

The leading black-box detectors resample the model and flag answers it
does not reproduce \citep{manakul2023selfcheckgpt,kuhn2023semantic,farquhar2024detecting}.
That presumes hallucinations are stochastic. Fabricated user facts need not
be: an underdetermined question maps the same context to the same guess
every time. More fundamentally, two users who share a conversation (the same intake answers and, where the agent is shown them, the same reactions) are indistinguishable to the model, so nothing computed from the model and its context can tell which of them it is wrong about (Theorem~\ref{thm:bound}). Context can still reveal \emph{population}
risk, how often a commitment is wrong among users with the same intake, and we show that a well-calibrated context-only predictor can
exploit it. Sampling-consistency scores of a self-consistent agent do not even do that (Corollary~\ref{cor:degenerate}).

The way out is to look at the user rather than the model. In USHER the agent
must externalize each assumption as a \emph{commitment}
$(\text{attribute},\text{value},p)$, and the user's reaction (accepting it,
contradicting it, or ignoring it) is recorded. Reactions are noisy and partial, but they depend on the truth for \emph{this} user, which no resample does: under a simple model the posterior of the hallucination event uses them exactly (Proposition~\ref{prop:posterior}), and, with the agent's trajectory held fixed, less noisy reactions cannot hurt (Theorem~\ref{thm:feedback}). Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Why resampling fails, and by how much.} No detector computed
from the model and its context (resampling, self-verification, hidden-state
probes) can tell which of two users sharing a conversation it is wrong about
(Theorem~\ref{thm:bound}), and a dispersion score's AUROC lies within
$\tfrac12e_0e_1$ of $\tfrac12+\tfrac12(e_1-e_0)$, where $e_1$ and $e_0$ are
how often resamples disagree with wrong and with right commitments
(Corollary~\ref{cor:degenerate}). On fresh sessions
from \pt{C/derived/L_U/nagents} agents, all three resamples agree on
\pt{C/PC/range/all_agree}\% of the commitments whose truth depends on
undisclosed facts, resample disagreement predicts their errors at
\pt{C/PC/range/auroc_disagreement} AUROC, and SelfCheckGPT and semantic
entropy score \pt{C/L_U/pooled/H1: AUROC(SelfCheckGPT)} and
\pt{C/L_U/pooled/H1: AUROC(semantic_entropy)} on user-specific
hallucination.
\item \textbf{A protocol that makes these errors detectable.} In USHER the
agent states each assumption as a commitment with a confidence and the
user's implicit reaction is recorded. Detectors on these observables beat
both sampling detectors by \pt{C/L_U/pooled/H4: V6 - SelfCheckGPT} to
\pt{C/L_U/pooled/H4b: ctx_lr+fb - semantic_entropy} AUROC pooled, and the
user's reactions add detectable signal beyond population base rates (\pt{C/L_U/pooled/H2: V6 - V6-prior};
significant for \pt{C/derived/L_U/npos/V6 - V6-prior} agents).
\item \textbf{A posterior that says which claim is wrong, and what to ask.} Our label-event
posterior is exact under a conditional-independence model, and with a
symmetric channel its priors are identified from reactions alone up to one
noise parameter (Proposition~\ref{prop:identify}). Its intake-aware form, V7, learns which
commitments the intake leaves to the user. Designed on development data
after the registered results and registered before its first run, it
reaches \pt{V/hc/L_U/pooled/--: AUROC(V7)} AUROC and beats the supervised
detector on the same raw inputs (registered H8:
\pt{V/hc/L_U/pooled/H8: V7 - intake_lr+fb}); a classifier handed its
structure matches it, and its gain is smallest on Mistral-24B. The user's
reactions add \pt{S/pooled/V7 - V7-prior} to it (exploratory). Per commitment it identifies
the wrong user-specific ones at \pt{PC/user_dependent/pooled/auroc_V7} AUROC
with calibration error \pt{PC/user_dependent/pooled/ece_V7}, against
\pt{PC/user_dependent/pooled/auroc_reaction} for the commitment's own reaction
and \pt{PC/user_dependent/pooled/auroc_disagreement} for resample
disagreement. \chk{After the registered V6 picker failed, an amendment (H9)
tested V7 as the picker: one question about its riskiest commitment removes
\val{CL7/reduction/V7 posterior@1}\% of the agent's wrong confident
user-specific commitments, and beats a random question
(\val{CL7/d/wcu/H9: V7 posterior@1 - random@1} per session).}
\item \textbf{Where the information is.} Exact Bayes predictors in a
simulator with known truth show that sampling fails not for lack of
information: the population risk of the commitments alone reaches
\val{C/RA/range/eta0 (Bayes, no feedback)} AUROC, the reactions add
\pt{C/RA/pooled/H5: etaF (Bayes, feedback) - eta0 (Bayes, no feedback)}
beyond it, and nearly all of that reaches the agent's transcript.
\item \textbf{A pre-registered evaluation, fully reported.} After a blind
internal audit of our development results we fixed a protocol bug,
registered every comparison and its wording, and generated fresh sessions
with five agents from four families and two domains. We report every
registered outcome, including those against our first posterior and a
simulated patient that failed its quality gate, and, exploratory, a second
simulated patient on whose weaker reactions V7 trails a supervised detector.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Hallucination detection.} Sampling-based detectors measure
agreement across resamples \citep{manakul2023selfcheckgpt,kuhn2023semantic,farquhar2024detecting};
semantic entropy is explicitly scoped to stochastic confabulations, not
consistent errors \citep{farquhar2024detecting}. Learned confidence models
from a model's generations \citep{ulmer2024apricot}, white-box probes
\citep{azaria2023internal,chen2024inside,kossen2024sep}, and verification by
the model's own questions \citep{dhuliawala2024cove} or by search
\citep{wei2024longform} use nothing about the user beyond the context, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:bound} applies to them. Consistent errors are documented
in snowballing \citep{zhang2024snowball} and self-contradiction studies
\citep{mundler2024selfcontradictory}, and calibrated models must
hallucinate on facts absent from training \citep{kalai2024calibrated}; our
setting is the per-user analogue. Verbalized confidence
\citep{kadavath2022language,tian2023just,xiong2024can} is a commitment-level
signal we include as a baseline, and iterative prompting can separate
epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty \citep{yadkori2024believe}, still from
the model given its context.

\paragraph{Hallucination in agents.} MIRAGE-Bench \citep{zhang2025mirage}
taxonomizes agent actions unfaithful to instructions, history or
observations; CAR-bench \citep{kirmayr2026carbench} tests whether agents
admit missing information with a simulated user; AgentHallu
\citep{liu2026agenthallu} localizes the step that introduced an error.
In multiagent systems a commitment is a public, checkable obligation of one
agent to another \citep{singh1999ontology}, and a probabilistic commitment
binds an agent to achieve an outcome with a stated probability
\citep{zhang2016commitment}; ours are probabilistic assertions about the
counterpart's private state, checked against its reactions. Dialogue models
hallucinate about their partner as well as about the world
\citep{dziri2022origin}. We study detection when the
missing information is the user's own.

\paragraph{Clarification.} When to ask has been framed through expected
value of perfect information \citep{rao2018learning,suri2025sage}, ambiguity
detection \citep{zhang2025clarify}, conformal prediction \citep{ren2023knowno},
and information-gain rewards on $\tau$-bench
\citep{deng2026clarification,yao2024taubench}. In clinical dialogue, MediQ
\citep{li2024mediq} and Uncertainty of Thoughts \citep{hu2024uot} ask a
simulated patient questions to reduce the doctor model's uncertainty. These
reason about the agent's uncertainty before it acts; we use post-hoc
commitment risk to choose which assertions to confirm, and measure the
effect with the agent re-reasoning.

\paragraph{Implicit and noisy feedback.} Implicit signals such as clicks are
informative but biased \citep{joachims2005clickthrough}. Conversation partners ground what they assert through
the other's acceptance \citep{clark1991grounding}; spoken dialogue
systems confirm hypotheses implicitly and update beliefs from the user's
reaction \citep{bohus2005constructing,williams2007pomdp}, and chatbots can
learn from their partner's satisfaction signals \citep{hancock2019learning}; our reactions play
the same role for asserted user facts. The general channel of our posterior,
with error-dependent contradiction and confirmation rates, has the form of
the guess and slip parameters of knowledge tracing
\citep{corbett1994knowledge}. Our estimation step
inverts a noise channel, as in learning from noisy labels
\citep{angluin1988noisy,natarajan2013learning,scott2013asymmetric} and
annotator models \citep{dawid1979maximum}. Agents that revise under user
pushback raise sycophancy concerns \citep{sharma2024sycophancy}; our
simulated user follows a long line of user simulation for dialogue
\citep{schatzmann2006survey}.

\section{Problem, Protocol, and Labels}
\label{sec:protocol}

\begin{definition}[User-specific hallucination]
A \emph{commitment} asserts a value $v$ for an attribute $a$ of the current
user with confidence $p\in[0,1]$. It is \emph{user-dependent} if its true
value is not determined by what the user has disclosed, and it is a
user-specific hallucination if it is user-dependent, confident
($p\ge\tau$), and wrong.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Protocol.} An USHER session has four stages. (i)~\emph{Intake}: the
user answers a few coarse questions; the agent sees only these answers.
(ii)~\emph{Commitment}: at each turn the agent emits one commitment in a
fixed format, \texttt{[attribute]: value, p=confidence}. (iii)~\emph{Reaction}:
the user's implicit reaction $F_t\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ (contradicts, no reaction,
accepts) is recorded and shown to the agent for its next six turns.
(iv)~\emph{Detection and intervention}: a detector scores the session and
its commitments; high-risk commitments trigger a confirmation question.

\paragraph{Labels.} With confident set $H=\{t:p_t\ge\tau\}$, $\tau=0.6$, the \emph{composite} label $L$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:label}) marks a session in which at least half of the confident commitments are wrong ($\rho=\tfrac12$), and is $0$ if there are none. It mixes two kinds of
error. Some attributes are determined by the intake through rules the agent
was never shown (e.g.\ contrast is needed iff urgency is medium or high);
errors there are guesses about the clinic, not about the user. The
\emph{user-specific} label $\LU$ applies the same rule to the confident
commitments that are user-dependent in that session, and is $0$ when there
are none. Whether a commitment is user-dependent is decided per session by
enumerating the hidden profile's support: \texttt{scan\_safe\_now}, for
example, depends on days since treatment only when a treatment was recent;
\texttt{needs\_contrast} is fixed by urgency, so a wrong ``no'' counts in
$L$ but never in $\LU$.
$\LU$ is our primary label; $L$ is reported as secondary.

\section{Detectors}
\label{sec:detectors}

The protocol yields two observables per turn, a commitment and a reaction. We read
them with two kinds of \emph{USHER detectors}: a label-event posterior, which
also gives per-commitment risk, and supervised classifiers. The sampling
baselines read only the model and its context. Table~\ref{tab:access} lists what each one sees.

\paragraph{USHER posterior.} The score is a plug-in estimate of the posterior
of Proposition~\ref{prop:posterior}. Each confident commitment falls in a
cell $\kappa=(\text{attribute},\text{value},\text{confidence bucket})$
(buckets $[0.6,0.65)$, $[0.65,0.75)$, $[0.75,1]$); cell priors come from
training reactions by the method of moments
(Proposition~\ref{prop:identify}), shrunk toward attribute- and bucket-level
estimates ($k=5$). The registered primary detector, V6, uses a general
channel: contradiction rates $r_w=P(F=-1\mid w)$ chosen on training labels
($r_1>r_0+0.1$), priors $\pi=(\text{rate}-r_0)/(r_1-r_0)$, and confirmation
rates fit by least squares; V3 is the symmetric special case. For $\LU$ the
event is restricted to attributes that depend on a hidden field anywhere in
the schema, which the detector can know without the clinic's rules; V6 is
therefore the posterior of a proxy event ($L_A$ in Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
Whether a commitment is wrong also depends on the intake, which the cell
omits, so (A1) fails and the plug-in is misspecified. V6$-$prior keeps the
fitted priors and ignores the test user's reactions.

\paragraph{Intake-aware posterior (V7).} V7 repairs both mismatches and
nothing else. Its cells add the intake answers each attribute depends on in
the schema, shrunk toward V6's cell (errors still share hidden facts across attributes, so (A1) remains an approximation).
Its label event is learned: a commitment whose truth the intake fixes is
wrong for every user with that intake or for none, a rule error rather than a
user-specific one. Starting from the schema-level set, V7 removes an
(attribute, intake) cell from the event, or returns it, when that lowers the
training log-loss, alternating with the channel fit; for $L$, whose event is
every confident commitment, only the cells change. On development data the intake cells alone add nothing on
$\LU$; the gain there comes from the learned event. V7 was designed after the registered
results were known, tuned on the development corpora only (twelve variants,
all logged), and frozen and registered (Amendment~6) before its first run on
confirmatory data, when the scores of its H8 comparator (intake-LR+F, below)
on four agents were already known.

\paragraph{Supervised USHER classifiers.} \emph{ctx-LR} is an $L_2$ logistic
regression ($C=0.1$, fixed) on counts of confident commitments per
(attribute, value) cell, $|H|$, the number of turns and mean confidence, with
no reactions and no samples; \emph{ctx-LR+F} adds counts per (attribute,
value, reaction) cell. Neither sees the intake answers; ``context-only'' in
the registered wording of H3 means no reactions and no samples. Both need
the structured commitments that USHER elicits. Two feedback statistics
(fraction of contradicted confident commitments, fraction of contradicted
turns) are the simplest USHER detectors.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Environment.} A simulated imaging clinic. Five coarse intake
answers are shown to the agent; four hidden fields (days since treatment,
allergy severity, kidney function, anxiety) are not. Each session asks for
6--12 of 12 attributes in dependency order. Six attributes can depend on a hidden field, but only for some intakes (a seventh, \texttt{transport\_assistance}, depends on one in the schema but is always fixed by the intake). Ground truth is a deterministic function of intake and hidden
fields; the hidden fields are independent draws from a fixed prior. The
agent is a real LLM; only the patient is simulated. A second domain, a
travel-booking assistant, has the same structure with different content:
five intake answers, four undisclosed traveller facts (such as mobility and
dietary needs), and twelve booking attributes, six of which can depend on
them. It was specified and registered before
any of its data existed.

\paragraph{Reactions.} In the main corpora the reaction to a commitment is
its correctness bit flipped with probability $\varepsilon=0.25$, absent for
two attributes the patient never reacts to. In the \emph{LLM-patient}
corpus a second model (Llama-3.1-8B; GPT-4o-mini in the second version) role-plays the patient: it is given
its own history but not the clinic's decision, one of three personas
(attentive, trusting, anxious), and the agent's commitment as a hedged
plain-language sentence, and replies in one or two sentences, which the
agent reads verbatim. A labeller model (in the first version, the same 8B
model) that sees only the sentence and the reply labels it as agreement,
contradiction or neither.
An \emph{informed} variant also tells the patient the clinic's decision.
A registered gate guards this corpus: a 100-session pilot runs first, and
the full 600 sessions run only if contradictions are at least $1.5$ times as
frequent after wrong commitments as after correct ones.

\paragraph{Development and confirmation.} Method development used two
development corpora (Llama-3.3-70B, 600 sessions; Llama-3.1-8B, 200). A
blind internal audit of these results found that the agent had been shown a
corrupted feedback display, that resamples covered only five attributes,
and that a feedback-free classifier nearly matched the posterior on $L$.
Before generating new data we committed an analysis plan
\citep{nosek2018preregistration}: labels, detectors (V6 primary), every
comparison, the statistics, and the wording each outcome licenses. Seven
amendments followed: three before any confirmatory data was seen (the
supervised feedback increment, label-budget curves, the analyses of
Section~\ref{sec:exp-decomp}, H5's wording); the second domain, before any
of its data existed; a replacement simulated patient, after the first
failed its gate; V7 with H7 and H8, after the registered results and the
intake-LR+F scores for four agents were known and before V7's first
confirmatory run, when no result of V6, V7 or the supervised detectors on
the Llama-70B or travel corpora had been seen (on Llama-70B, the CoVe
baseline and the registered clarification run had been run); and V7 as the
question picker (H9), after the registered clarification test had failed
and before V7's first run as picker. The confirmatory
corpora use a fresh seed, show the agent the raw reaction, draw three
resamples on every turn, and cover five agents (Llama-3.3-70B,
Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-72B, GPT-4o-mini, Mistral-Small-3.1-24B), 600
sessions each (the Llama-70B run stopped at 375 sessions when API credit ran
out and was resumed with the same seed; no session was regenerated or
dropped).

\paragraph{Statistics.} Splits are 70/30 by session for three split seeds;
detectors are fit on the training split and score the test split once;
AUROC is averaged over seeds. Differences use a paired bootstrap (2,000
resamples) clustered by session, since test sets of different seeds
overlap. Per-agent differences are computed on the test rows pooled over the
three seeds, so they can differ in the third decimal from differences of the
tables' seed-mean AUROCs. Pooled estimates average per-agent differences,
with sessions resampled within agent. A difference is called significant when its 95\%
interval excludes zero; we do not correct for multiplicity, and intervals
are conditional on the fitted detectors. Because the agents share sessions,
we also resample sessions jointly across agents and report Holm-adjusted
$p$-values (Table~\ref{tab:hyp}). The primary
hypotheses are in Table~\ref{tab:hyp}; per-agent intervals for every
registered comparison are in the repository's analysis output.

\paragraph{Oracle-information analyses.} Because the simulator's rules and hidden-field prior are known, we can compute three exact quantities. The \emph{population risk} $\bar\eta=\sum_h P(h)\,\LU(C,h)$ is the probability that the session's commitments would be wrong for a random user with the same intake. $\eta_X=P(\LU=1\mid X,C)$ adds the reactions the agent was shown (all but the last); by Theorem~\ref{thm:bound} it bounds every detector computed from the agent's context and its resamples. $\eta_F$ adds the last reaction and bounds every detector of commitments and reactions. The agent's policy does not involve the hidden facts, so its factors cancel and no agent model is needed. These are not
deployable detectors; they locate the information. We also give V6 and
ctx-LR(+F) the same oracle knowledge of which commitments are
user-dependent in each session (V6-session, LR-session). (The registered
plan calls $\bar\eta$, $\eta_X$ and $\eta_F$ eta0, etaX and etaF.)

Table~\ref{tab:percommit} shows the mechanism. \chk{On commitments whose
truth depends on undisclosed facts, resamples disagree about as often when the agent is right as when it is
wrong: $e_1$ and $e_0$ differ by at most \val{C/PC/maxdiff_e} on every
agent, so by Corollary~\ref{cor:degenerate} the AUROC of disagreement is
centred within half that of $\tfrac12$. Llama-70B, Qwen-72B and GPT-4o-mini
mostly repeat themselves; Llama-8B and Mistral-24B disagree with themselves
often, but nearly as often when right as when wrong. One reaction from our simulated
user gives $0.75$, as designed; the comparison shows how little a user's
reaction needs to carry to beat resampling, not how much a real user's
does.} At the session level
(Table~\ref{tab:main}), SelfCheckGPT and semantic entropy score
\val{C/L_U/pooled/H1: AUROC(SelfCheckGPT)} and
\val{C/L_U/pooled/H1: AUROC(semantic_entropy)} pooled over agents, both

detectors are near chance on user-specific hallucination. Resampling sessions jointly across agents, which share them, gives
\val{J/L_U/H1: AUROC(SelfCheckGPT)} and
\val{J/L_U/H1: AUROC(semantic_entropy)}, \val{J/L_U/H1band}. Per agent, SelfCheckGPT ranges over \val{C/L_U/range/SelfCheckGPT};
\chk{the top value (\val{C/derived/L_U/argmax/SelfCheckGPT}) lies just
outside the band.} Chain-of-Verification on Llama-70B, the model verifying itself, scores
\val{C/L_U/70B/cove} (V6 $-$ CoVe \val{C/L_U/70B/V6 - cove}). Sampling is not blind everywhere: on the composite label,
where many errors are guesses about clinic rules that do vary across
resamples, SelfCheckGPT reaches \val{C/L/pooled/H1: AUROC(SelfCheckGPT)}. The failure is specific to facts the user never disclosed.

\subsection{Robustness to the user's behaviour}
\label{sec:exp-robust}

Our users react with a designed noise level. To see how much that matters,
we regenerated the reactions seen by the detectors on the confirmatory
trajectories, with the agents' trajectories fixed (Theorem~\ref{thm:feedback}'s
setting; exploratory; mean over agents of \val{E/draws} draws). \chk{As the
flip rate rises from $0.05$ to $0.45$, V7's mean AUROC on $\LU$ falls from
\val{E/mean/eps=0.05,rho=1.0/V7} to \val{E/mean/eps=0.45,rho=1.0/V7}, faster
than the Bayes predictor given the intake, the commitments and the same
regenerated reactions (\val{E/mean/eps=0.05,rho=1.0/etaF (oracle)} to
\val{E/mean/eps=0.45,rho=1.0/etaF (oracle)}), against
\val{E/mean/eps=0.05,rho=1.0/intake_lr+fb} to
\val{E/mean/eps=0.45,rho=1.0/intake_lr+fb} for intake-LR+F. With reactions to
half or a quarter of the commitments, V7 scores
\val{E/mean/eps=0.25,rho=0.5/V7} and \val{E/mean/eps=0.25,rho=0.25/V7}
(intake-LR+F: \val{E/mean/eps=0.25,rho=0.5/intake_lr+fb} and

\section{Limitations}

\emph{Simulation.} The agents are real but the user is simulated. In the
main corpora it knows whether each commitment is correct with probability
$0.75$; real users know their facts, not the clinic's rules, which the LLM
patient tests; with its weaker reactions V7 loses its advantage
(Section~\ref{sec:exp-patient}). The oracle analyses rely on the simulator's known rules and prior, which a
deployment does not have. \emph{Two synthetic domains.} Both private-attribute domains are
simulators we designed, with deterministic rules and independent hidden
facts; real user populations have correlated, partially self-reported
facts.
\emph{Post-hoc method.} V7 was designed after the registered results
were known, tuned on development data only, and registered before its first
confirmatory run; Llama-70B and the travel domain are the corpora on which no result of the
compared detectors had been seen when it was registered. H9 was registered
after the first clarification test failed; the loop covers one agent, 120
sessions and a simulated patient that answers questions truthfully. \emph{Labels.} Labels are programmatic and noise-free by construction; there
is no human evaluation. \emph{Supervision.} The posterior and the supervised
baselines are fit on labelled training sessions; the posterior can run
without labels when the channel is known, which we only test in
development. \emph{Untested detectors.} Our LLM judge is zero-shot; a judge trained on
labelled transcripts also reads the reactions and is not bounded by
Theorem~\ref{thm:bound}. Sampling detectors used three resamples (ten were
registered as optional and not run), and with exact matching semantic
entropy takes few values. We did not run hidden-state probes;
Theorem~\ref{thm:bound} predicts they share the within-context limit, but
they could exploit population risk as ctx-LR does. \emph{Theory.} Proposition~\ref{prop:posterior} is exact only under (A1)--(A2), and our detectors are plug-in estimates; Theorem~\ref{thm:feedback} holds only with the agent's trajectories fixed.

\section{Conclusion}

When an agent invents a fact about its user, its own samples cannot tell which of the users sharing its context it is wrong about. Some of the risk is visible in what the agent commits to; the rest is visible only through the user's reactions. Making the agent state
its assumptions as probabilistic commitments and recording the user's
reactions makes both available, and a Bayes analysis in a simulator with
known truth shows how much each is worth. An intake-aware posterior turns
them into session and per-commitment risk; the structure it encodes, which
commitments the intake leaves to the user, is what separates the detectors
we tested. In our simulated closed loop, confirming the commitment its risk ranks
highest removes more user-specific errors than a random question.
